01 · the interrogation

A −7.1% "signal" walks into a robustness battery

The first event study said on-market director sales preceded −7.1% abnormal returns (z = −7.5). Instead of publishing that, the study spent a week attacking it: deduplicating serial sellers, swapping return models, matching controls, buying exact earnings dates, adjusting for sectors, purging retirees. Most of the magnitude was self-inflicted artifact — but a core refused to die: large ($1M+), freely-timed director sales, roughly −5–6% per quarter, and it got cleaner under attack.

02 · the survivor

Thirty-one sales, one stubborn drift

The surviving cell: on-market sales over $1M, filed more than 30 days after the company's last results — free-choice trades, exactly where insider information should live. 21 of 31 underperformed their sector over the next quarter. It is not front-running bad earnings (the next results were, if anything, better than average), not a sector artifact, and not retirement liquidations. It is a diffuse ~−5–6%/quarter idiosyncratic drift with no identified mechanism — which is precisely why it goes to a pre-registered forward test on a paper account, not a trading account.

Anatomy of the scariest data point

The worst event — Catalyst Metals, −43% after a director's $9M sale — looked like the smoking gun. Forensics said otherwise: the crash was a gold-sector drawdown (every big down day was sector-wide; the worst, −14.4%, had no company news at all), and the company's next result was NPAT +40%. But even against its own gold peers, CYL still underperformed by ~20% — the sale preceded real idiosyncratic damage and a sector slide the index-adjusted number wrongly bundled in. This one chart is why the whole cohort got re-scored sector-adjusted.

03 · the graveyard

Five popular anomalies, tested and buried

Each test below was specified — cohort, endpoint, success threshold — before it ran, with a shared multiple-testing correction. Honest nulls are the study's main product: they are what make the one survivor credible rather than cherry-picked.

∅ NULL

Director purchases

1,254 buys: +0.45% (z=+0.12). Placebo-confirmed nothing, at every horizon. Directors buy dips; the dips don't care.

⚠ INCONCLUSIVE

Post-earnings drift (PEAD)

712 results days. Best-vs-worst reaction quintile spread +3.7% (t=1.5), sign flips across seasons. Big misses show zero drift — bad news is priced same-day.

× REFUTED

Dividend-cut drift

105 cuts vs 82 holds: spread +0.1% (t=0.04). A cut is fully priced the morning it lands.

× REFUTED

Insider dip-buying

Directors bought after 45% of results-day crashes — near-reflexive. Bought crashes recovered no better than ignored ones (−0.1% vs +1.7%).

∅ NOTHING

CEO / MD changes

63 appointments & cessations: both indistinguishable from base drift. One anecdote (WTC −41%), no pattern.

× DEAD

The exec-seller theory

Executive vs non-executive seller adds nothing — and the LLM role labels it rested on were only 72% right vs primary documents.

04 · market plumbing

The exchange's "price sensitive" flag misses what moves prices

Director-trade notices are never flagged price-sensitive on the ASX. Yet a $1M+ insider sale moves its stock 2.8% on filing day — double the baseline for the same form type, a fifth of them beyond 3%. The market demonstrably prices a category the exchange's materiality flag ignores.

05 · the LLM under the microscope

When two documents describe one result, they disagree 28% of the time

Most results events arrive as several documents — the statutory 4D, a media release, an investor deck. Extracting all of them independently creates a free, golden-label-less audit: do the documents agree? Only 45% of field pairs match exactly. EPS is the disaster zone — basic vs diluted, cents vs dollars, statutory vs underlying. And the model's self-reported confidence barely notices: 0.962 on consensus values vs 0.951 on deviant ones. Convention errors feel exactly like correct answers from the inside.

How the extractor earned trust: eval-driven prompt development

No bulk extraction ran on an unbenchmarked prompt. Each prompt version was scored against hand-labeled goldens before touching the corpus — including the iteration where the goldens themselves turned out to be wrong (v4 and v5 regressed and were binned; the director-trades jump came partly from fixing labels the eval exposed).

Three lessons that generalize beyond this study

LESSON 01

Your golden labels can be the bug

The eval flagged the model 15.6% on director roles. Investigation showed 30 of 36 golden labels named roles that appear nowhere in the documents — labeled from memory, not text. The model was right; the labels were wrong. Accuracy jumped 75%→93% across two honest prompt iterations.

LESSON 02

LLM world-knowledge enrichment fails predictably

Asking a model "what role does this person hold?" was only 72% right vs primary filings — famous-elsewhere executives, post-cutoff CEOs, time-dependent roles. $1 of extraction from actual appointment notices caught every failure mode.

LESSON 03

Confidence scores don't see convention errors

Per-field confidence separates "couldn't read it" from "read it fine" — but not "read the wrong convention". Cross-document disagreement is the cheap detector goldens can't be: it scales with the corpus and costs nothing.

06 · what would make this real

In-sample survival is not an edge

The surviving signal was found in this data, then re-cut repeatedly — every printed t-statistic is optimistic. So the study does what the checklist says: the hypothesis is frozen, and from here it is judged only on filings it has never seen — a pre-registered forward test running through mid-2027 on a paper account. Confirmed or refuted, the result gets published with equal prominence.

Read the fine print — it's the point

  • In-sample throughout. 2024–26 discovery window; heavy accumulated multiple-testing debt, partially controlled by pre-specified endpoints and Bonferroni families.
  • Prototype prices. yfinance daily closes; today's ASX 200 constituents (survivorship lifts every baseline — visible in the data). Delisting-aware data gates anything published as a backtest.
  • No consensus estimates. "Surprise" is proxied by the market's own reaction; reported YoY changes explain almost none of it (ρ=0.12).
  • Small n. The surviving cohort is 31 events. One bad quarter of luck could produce it.
  • Paper trading only. Simulated fills are optimistic; backtest ≠ live; no real capital, ever.
